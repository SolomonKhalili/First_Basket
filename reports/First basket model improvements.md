# Squeeze more signal from every game

The biggest gain left is **getting more learning out of the same ~1,500 games**, not adding new stats. The two best untried ideas both work on the player step (stage 2). First, teach the model the *order* of each team's first 2-3 scorers, not just the first one. Second, tune how hard each stat gets pulled toward the league average from the data, instead of picking that amount by hand. For first team to score, the one clear untried signal is in our own data: **the tip winner scores first 68.9% of the time at home but only 62.9% on the road**. For the tip itself, the cheap fixes are faster learning for new jumpers and dropping mid-game jump balls from their records. Expect small gains. Our model already matches the best public numbers: a **17.7% top-pick hit rate** for StatsBench vs our 16-20% ([StatsBench](https://blog.statsbench.com/first-basket-stats-nba-data/)). First team to score has a hard limit of **about 55-58% right**, however good the model is. One big outside fact: **on Oct 16, 2026 the NBA told its partner sportsbooks to stop offering "first-of" bets, including first basket and the opening tip**, starting this season ([AP via KSAT](https://www.ksat.com/sports/2026/10/16/some-wagers-certain-parlays-no-longer-can-be-offered-to-bettors-nba-tells-sportsbooks/)). Better guesses can still sharpen the site's picks, but there may be far fewer places to bet on them.

## We are already near the top, and the market just shrank

No one publishes a stronger first-basket model than ours. The only public backtest (a test on past games) is StatsBench's: **17.7% top-pick hits over 724 games in 2024-25**. It doesn't say how it was built or whether it was tested on games it hadn't seen ([StatsBench](https://blog.statsbench.com/first-basket-stats-nba-data/)). StatsBench uses the same two steps as us: first the tip, then each player's chance once his team has the ball. It also says the single most likely player on a slate "rarely clears 20%" ([StatsBench method](https://blog.statsbench.com/first-basket-predictions-data-method/)). With ~1,500 games, our own hit rate wobbles by about ±1 point from luck alone. So a 1-2 point gap between us and StatsBench means nothing. One open-source NBA first-basket project tested its upgrades on games it hadn't seen. Its best upgrade moved log-loss (a "how surprised was the model" score, lower is better) by only **about 0.006**, from 2.2777 to 2.2718. The book's opening price scored 2.3845 ([GitHub PR #176](https://github.com/OnyxJordan/onyx-baseball-fix/pull/176)). That is the size of win to expect from any single idea below.

The betting backdrop changed this season. The NBA's new rules took effect on opening night, Oct 20, 2026 ([Dimers](https://www.dimers.com/nba/news/nba-player-props-bans-regulated-betting-2026-27); [Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/nba-bans-certain-bet-types-ahead-of-new-season-oct-16-2026)). NBA partner books reportedly hold over 99% of the US market. No one has confirmed whether offshore books or books outside the deal must follow the rules ([Sportsbook Review](https://www.sportsbookreview.com/news/nba-restricts-player-props-and-parlays-following-betting-scandals-oct-20-2026/)). Even before the rules, these bets carried a big house cut. A 10-player board often adds up to **about 124%** instead of 100% ([PropsEdge](https://www.propsedge.io/blog/nba-first-basket-strategy)). At that cut, a 17-18% top pick only pays off at odds of about +500 or longer, and +500 needs a 16.7% hit rate just to break even ([StatsBench](https://blog.statsbench.com/first-basket-stats-nba-data/)). One study says books overprice stars by **about 3-5 points** ([HeatCheck](https://heatcheckhq.io/blog/nba-first-basket-data-study-2026)). So any real edge more likely sits with mid-priced non-stars than with the top pick.

## Ranked: what to try next and how to test it

Every test below uses our existing rule: train week by week only on games before the test week, then compare log-loss in both test periods. Keep a change only if it helps in both. "Expected gain" is a judgment call based on the sources, not a measured number.

| Rank | Idea (plain words) | Which step | Expected gain | How we'd test it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Learn from the order of the first 2-3 scorers**, not just the first | Player | Medium (the best-backed idea) | For each team, list its starters in the order they first score. Fit a "ranked choice" version of our player model (a rank-ordered logit) where later places count less (weights like 1, 0.5, 0.25). Tune those weights. Grade only on the real first basket. |
| 2 | **Let the data decide how much to pull each stat toward average** (data-tuned shrinking) | Player | Small-medium | For each count stat (season and last-10 first-basket rates, and so on), fit the "pretend extra games" amount from the training weeks, in place of our hand-picked number. Then try pulling each player toward a guess built from his usage and position, not the league average. |
| 3 | **Home-court term on the first-team step** | First team | Small, but our own data shows it | Add a home flag next to the tip chance in stage 1. First check whether the calibration stretch already captures the 68.9% vs 62.9% gap. |
| 4 | **Use first shot attempts as a second thing to predict** (not as a feature) | Player | Small-medium | Train one shared player model on two stacked lists: "who took the team's first shot" and "who made the first basket." Allow a few coefficients to differ between them. Every team takes a first shot every game, so this roughly doubles the clean examples. |
| 5 | **A per-player "personal bonus" in the player model** | Player | Small | Give each player his own small extra bump, held near zero by a penalty, with the penalty strength tuned on past weeks. This works like a simple version of a fancy multi-level model. |
| 6 | **Lineup-change and trade handling** | Player | Small overall, big on the games it touches | When a starter sits out, re-run stage 2 with the new starter and hand out the missing player's early-shot share. After a trade, carry over only part (try 0.3-0.5) of a player's old-team counts. Add "usage rank among tonight's five starters" as a feature. |
| 7 | **Faster learning for new jumpers** | Tip | Small | Raise the Elo step size (K, how fast a rating moves) for a jumper's first ~15 tips, then drop back to 24. One public tip model uses K=40 and a 15-game trial period. |
| 8 | **Drop mid-game jump balls from tip records** | Tip | Small | Rebuild tip rates from opening (and overtime) tips only, or count tie-ups at reduced weight, then compare. |
| 9 | **Temperature scaling** (one dial that makes all chances bolder or calmer) | Player and first team | Small but safe | Fit one number on past weeks that divides the player scores before they turn into chances. It never changes who is #1, only how sure the model is. |
| 10 | **Split by how the team got its first basket** | Player | Uncertain | Fit player shares separately for "scored on the opening play after winning the tip" and "scored after the ball changed hands." The opening play is usually drawn up for one player. |
| 11 | **Opening-possession shooter rate and team "one-guy" score** | Player | Uncertain | From play-by-play, count who shoots on the team's very first possession. Score how often each team goes to the same player, and lean on that player more for one-guy teams. Pool by coach, since the coach draws up the play. |
| 12 | **Equal-weight blend of 2-3 simple player models** | Player | Small | Average our model, a smaller-feature version, and a simple "shots^0.5 × first-shot share^0.25" model, each counted equally. Don't fit the blend weights. |
| 13 | **Add older seasons with a very slow fade** | Player | Small | Add 1-2 past seasons and try half-lives of 1-3 seasons, plus no fade at all. This is different from the rejected recency weighting: it adds data instead of cutting it. |
| 14 | **Block rate or reach as a starting guess for new jumpers** | Tip | Tiny | Only for jumpers with fewer than ~30 tips, start them from a guess based on block rate (and maybe combine reach + max vertical). Judge by log-loss and calibration, not hit rate. |
| 15 | **Starting-five scoring chance in a possession-chain formula** | First team | Tiny (about ±2 points at most) | Plug each side's heavily shrunk chance of scoring on a possession into the possession-chain formula described in the next section. |
| 16 | **Referee crew "coin-flip" factor** | Tip | Tiny | Pull the refs listed for each game, and check whether some crews push tip results toward 50/50. |

Ideas 1, 2, 4 and 5 all attack the same problem: one first basket per game is a thin signal. Horse-racing modelers faced the same issue and solved it by using the full finishing order. Bolton and Chapman fit a working model on just **200 races** this way ([Management Science](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v32y1986i8p1040-1060.html)). The ranked version of our model is the same math we already use, applied to more than one place ([Ragain & Ugander](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.05139)). It can be fit with free tools such as the R package PlackettLuce, which can also pull ratings toward average ([CRAN](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/PlackettLuce/refman/PlackettLuce.html)). The risk is that the 2nd and 3rd scorers reflect normal usage more than the drawn-up opening play. That is why the later places get smaller weights, and we tune those weights.

Soccer offers the case for idea 4. Shots predict a striker's future goals better than past goals do, because goals are rare and noisy ([StatsBomb](https://blogarchive.statsbomb.com/articles/soccer/shots-and-key-passes-are-better-than-goals-and-assists/)). We already rejected first-shot share as a *feature*, likely because our early-shot share and pulled-in rates already carry that signal. Using first shots as extra *practice questions* is a different test. For idea 2, the classic baseball result says pulling small-sample rates toward the average beats raw rates ([Hanck & Arnold](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10182-021-00420-w)). Splitting a rate into parts and shrinking each part separately predicts better than shrinking the total ([Albert 2016](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bpj:jqsprt:v:12:y:2016:i:2:p:73-85:n:4)). For idea 9, a single "boldness dial" usually beats fancier fixes. The fancier method, isotonic calibration, overfits on small data, so skip it ([Guo et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html); [Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting)). For idea 12, simple equal-weight averages tend to beat fitted weights, because the fitted weights themselves are noisy ([Qian et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:gam:jecnmx:v:7:y:2019:i:3:p:39-:d:265946)). Benter, the most famous betting modeler, also warns that adding stats one at a time by trial and error builds "a hodgepodge" of overlapping inputs ([Cydalytics summary](https://cydalytics.blogspot.com/2020/01/bill-benter-1994-computer-based-horse.html)). So where possible, swap a stat for a better version of itself instead of stacking another one on top.

## First team to score is capped near 58%, so chase calibration

Our own database (1,537 games) says the tip winner scored first **66.0%** of the time, counting free throws, and **64.1%** counting field goals only. Both seasons matched. A 27,000-tip study from 2000-2021 found **64%** ([Dickinson](https://dickinson.edu/news/article/6001/dickinson_research_reveals_a_new_take_on_nba_tipoffs)). The missing third is mostly simple possession math. In our data, a team's first shot goes in only about **46-47%** of the time, so the tip loser often gets the ball back. If a team scores on about 48.5% of its trips, a back-and-forth game produces almost exactly 66%.

That math sets a ceiling. Only about **32%** of any change in tip chance carries through to the chance of scoring first. Even a tip call made with 75% confidence gives only about a 58% shot at the first-team pick. Typical games sit at 40-70% tip confidence, so **first-team accuracy tops out around 55-58%**, and we are likely already there. The better target is log-loss and calibration, meaning how honest our percentages are. Our local benchmark: DraftKings' last price picked the first-scoring team **59.7%** of the time, with log-loss **0.673** over 308 games (a coin flip scores 0.693) and a **7.6%** house cut. With so few games, compare against DraftKings on log-loss over the same games, not on hit rate.

Team "scores first beyond the tip" habits are mostly noise in our data. Odd-game vs even-game results lined up only weakly (r ≈ 0.14-0.15). The true spread between teams is about ±3 points, which matches our earlier rejection of team first-possession rates. An older tip model also found no real team differences and used one flat rate for everyone ([Galarneau GitHub](https://github.com/mattgalarneau/NBA-Jump-Balls)). Home court is the exception: after winning the tip, home teams score first **68.9%** of the time and road teams **62.9%**, about 2.5 standard errors apart. That makes idea 3 the top first-team fix. Our current home edge sits in the tip step, which may be the wrong place: an older sample found home teams won the opening tip only **50.5%** of the time ([82games](https://82games.com/random14.htm)). Test moving the home edge from the tip step to the "who scores after the tip" step. Also cheap: use play-by-play to check how often the tip winner actually gets the ball, and why the tip winner fails to score first (turnover, miss, or opponent rebound).

## The tip is about timing, so lean on track records

Tips are won mostly by timing and reaction to the ref's toss, not height. Yao Ming (7'6") won only about 42% of his tips. Shaq won 76%, and Andre Drummond trains his reaction time with tennis balls ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/nba/2024/2/21/24074472/jump-ball-rules-strategy-chicanery-history)). A 45,000-tip study found height matters mainly at big gaps (over about 6 inches). Rebounding and age showed no effect. Shot blocking was the only stat with a measured link, and a weak one (r² = 0.12) ([FanSided](https://fansided.com/2015/10/06/almost-time-for-tip-off-jump-ball-stats)). That explains why our height prior made the model overconfident. It also suggests block rate, or a "big mismatch only" flag, as a gentler starting guess for jumpers with few tips.

Track records swing a lot. Career leaders win about 70-76% and the worst about 22-27% ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/nba/2024/2/21/24074472/jump-ball-rules-strategy-chicanery-history)), so our approach of leaning on each jumper's history is right. Giannis's 76% is padded by mid-game tie-ups against smaller players, which supports idea 8. One public project compared three rating systems (Elo, Glicko-2, TrueSkill) and found Elo had the best log-loss. It used TrueSkill for live picks because TrueSkill learns faster on new jumpers ([EllAchE GitHub](https://github.com/ellache/nba-tipoff-scraper)). Idea 7 gets that speed while keeping Elo. No public source reports a tip-calling accuracy we can compare against, so our **65-68%** is at or above anything published. The real ceiling is likely near 70%, because most games pit two mid-level centers against each other. Refs add noise: the NBA has no standard toss, and one ref guesses 15-25% of tosses are bad ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/nba/2024/2/21/24074472/jump-ball-rules-strategy-chicanery-history)). But no one records who actually tossed, so a ref factor (idea 16) will likely fail the same way head-to-head did. A few coaches, such as Ty Lue, say they don't mind losing the tip (same source). That could explain some misses for those teams, but it is hard to test.

Some conflicting numbers are now settled by our own data. Betting sites claim the tip winner scores first anywhere from "55-58%" to 68% ([PropsEdge](https://www.propsedge.io/blog/nba-first-basket-strategy); [nbabettingrules](https://nbabettingrules.com/articles/nba-first-basket-betting/)). Our 66.0% (64.1% for field goals only) matches the big Dickinson study. Remember that books grade differently: FanDuel's First Basket counts free throws, while DraftKings settles its player market ("1st Points Scorer") and its "Team to Score 1st FG" on the first field goal, so free throws don't count there (checked against DraftKings' bet slips and house rules on 2026-11-19).

## Conclusion

The model is no longer short on ideas about basketball. It is short on *examples*. Every top-ranked idea finds more lessons in games we already have: later scorers, first shots, data-tuned shrinking, and a personal bonus per player. None of them needs new data sources. That is also why no single change will be dramatic: expect gains around a few thousandths of log-loss each. Two or three that pass the two-period test could add up to a real gain. First team to score is close to its natural limit, so the job there is honest percentages, starting with moving the home edge from the tip step to the "after the tip" step. The tip step needs only small tune-ups.

The bigger open question is not the model but the market. With NBA partner books told to drop first-of bets this season, we should check which books still offer these markets before spending much effort on betting-edge work. That includes comparing our chances against de-vigged book prices (prices with the house cut removed) by player tier. Improvements to the site's picks are still worth making either way.
